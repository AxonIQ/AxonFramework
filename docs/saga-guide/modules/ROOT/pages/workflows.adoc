= Workflows
:navtitle: Workflows
:reftext: Workflows

Every approach on the preceding pages builds a process out of parts you assemble yourself: an event handler, some form
of state, a command. Workflows take the opposite position. The process is written as a single method that reads
top to bottom, and the machinery that lets it survive a restart is provided rather than assembled.

*This is the recommended way to orchestrate a business process, and the first option to consider.* Orchestration is
what a workflow is for, and writing the flow as a flow removes most of what the other pages spend their length on. The
reason those pages exist is that a workflow is not available to everyone:

* Workflows are part of Axoniq Framework, not Axon Framework, and require an Axoniq licence for production use.
* They are available as a preview rather than a production release.

If either of those rules them out for you, pick one of the approaches on the preceding pages. They are complete
solutions using nothing beyond Axon Framework, not stopgaps waiting for a licence.

Consult the
link:https://docs.axoniq.io/axon-framework-reference/development/workflows/[Workflows documentation,role=external,window=_blank]
for the API and current status.

== What changes

The approaches on the other pages are all *reactive*. Each event arrives independently, the process reconstructs
enough context to decide what to do, and the flow between steps exists only in the reader's head or on an event model.
That is why so much of this guide is about state: reconstructing context is the work.

A workflow inverts that. The sequence is written as a sequence, and waiting is an ordinary statement rather than the
end of one handler and the start of another. Where a reactive process spreads "ask for payment, then wait, then
approve or reject" across three handlers and a stored correlation, a workflow expresses it as three lines.

That is the headline: the code is close to ordinary imperative code, in a place where the framework usually asks you
to think in reactions. What you give up is not correctness but choice, since the persistence of the flow's position is
no longer yours to design.

The consequences follow from that inversion:

* *The process is legible.* One method describes the whole flow, which is what people usually want when they ask where
  the process is.
* *State stops being your problem.* No repository, no derived model, no process events. The position within the
  flow is the state, and it is persisted for you.
* *Waiting becomes a first-class concept.* A timeout is part of the flow rather than a projection and a sweep, so
  xref::deadlines.adoc[the deadline pattern] is not needed.
* *You take on a dependency and a licence.* This is the trade, and it is the reason the other four approaches exist.

== When it is the right answer

Whenever it is available to you, and especially when the process itself is the complicated part: many steps, real
branching, several compensating paths, or waiting that matters. Those are the cases where the reactive approaches
spread one idea thinly across many files and the shape becomes hard to see.

Availability is the question that decides it in practice, and it decides it before any judgement about the process:

* If you cannot take an Axoniq licence, the question is settled. The other approaches need nothing beyond Axon
  Framework.
* If you are on Axon Framework rather than Axoniq Framework, workflows are not available to you today, and something
  from the preceding pages is what you will write.
* If a preview dependency is not acceptable in your production build, the same applies until the release lands.

Where a workflow is available, the one case for still choosing something else is a process small enough that the flow
was never in doubt. A rental that asks for payment and reacts to the answer is three reactions, and expressing it as a
workflow buys legibility it did not lack. The bike rental example used throughout these pages is deliberately at that
scale, which is why it is not written as a workflow here. That is a weak preference, not a rule: if you have workflows
and you like them, use them for the small processes too.

== Relationship to the other approaches

Workflows are not one more entry on the same list so much as a different axis. The four other approaches differ in
*where the process keeps its state*; a workflow differs in *who reconstructs the flow*.

That has a practical consequence worth planning for. A process written as
xref::automation-slices.adoc[automation slices] is the furthest from a workflow, because it deliberately has no centre;
converting it later means gathering the slices back into one flow. A process written as
xref::state-from-process-events.adoc[state from its own events] is the closest, because it already records its own
progress as facts, which is much the same information a workflow persists on your behalf.

If you expect to adopt workflows later, that is an argument for keeping the process in one class now.

== Replacing a process that is already running

If you are replacing an existing process rather than writing a new one, the
