= Workflows
:navtitle: Workflows
:reftext: Workflows

Every other approach in this guide builds a process out of parts you assemble yourself: an event handler, some form of
state, a command. Workflows take the opposite position. The process is written as a single method that reads
top to bottom, and the machinery that lets it survive a restart is provided rather than assembled.

*This is the recommended way to orchestrate a business process, and the first option to consider.* Orchestration is
what a workflow is for, and writing the flow as a flow removes most of what the other pages spend their length on.

The other pages exist because not everything that reacts to events is a process worth writing as a flow. A handful of
independent reactions is better modelled as a handful of independent reactions, and forcing it into one method buys a
centre it never needed. Where there genuinely is a flow, this is the page.

[NOTE]
====
Workflows are part of Axoniq Framework rather than Axon Framework, and are available as a preview rather than a
production release. Trying them out is free; running them in production needs an Axoniq licence.
====

Consult the
link:https://docs.axoniq.io/axon-framework-reference/development/workflows/[Workflows documentation,role=external,window=_blank]
for the API and current status.

== What changes

The approaches on the other pages are all *reactive*. Each event arrives independently, the process reconstructs
enough context to decide what to do, and the flow between steps exists only in the reader's head or on an event model.
That is why so much of this guide is about state: reconstructing context is the work.

A workflow inverts that. The sequence is written as a sequence, and waiting is an ordinary statement rather than the
end of one handler and the start of another. Where a reactive process spreads "ask for payment, then wait, then
approve or reject" across three handlers and a stored correlation, a workflow expresses it as three lines.

That is the headline: the code is close to ordinary imperative code, in a place where the framework usually asks you
to think in reactions. What you give up is not correctness but choice, since the persistence of the flow's position is
no longer yours to design.

The consequences follow from that inversion:

* *The process is legible.* One method describes the whole flow, which is what people usually want when they ask where
  the process is.
* *State stops being your problem.* No repository, no derived model, no process events. The position within the
  flow is the state, and it is persisted for you.
* *Waiting becomes a first-class concept.* A timeout is part of the flow rather than a projection and a sweep, so
  xref::deadlines.adoc[the deadline pattern] is not needed.
* *You take on a dependency.* Workflows live in Axoniq Framework, so the process is written against that rather than
  against Axon Framework alone.

== When it is the right answer

Whenever it is available to you. The recommendation does not depend on the size or shape of the process, and there is
no threshold below which one of the other approaches becomes the better choice.

The reason it holds for small processes too is that processes grow. Adding a step to a workflow is adding a line in the
middle of a method. Adding one to any of the other approaches means a new handler, a piece of state to carry the new
information, and a correlation to get right, all of it spread across files. A process written as a workflow starts
easier and stays easier, and the gap widens with every step.

What does decide against a workflow is the process not being one. If what you have is a set of reactions that happen to
share a subject, xref::vertical-slices.adoc[vertical slices] keep them independent, and a workflow would give them a
centre they do not need. That is a judgement about the shape of the work, not about the framework you are on.

Practically, a preview dependency has to be acceptable in your build, and production use needs a licence. Neither
affects trying it.

The bike rental example running through the rest of this guide is deliberately not written as a workflow. That is so
the other approaches can be compared line for line against the same process, not a suggestion that a process this small
is better off without one.

== Relationship to the other approaches

Workflows are not one more entry on the same list so much as a different axis. The other approaches differ in where the
process lives and how it keeps its state; a workflow differs in *who reconstructs the flow*.

That has a practical consequence worth planning for. A process written as
xref::vertical-slices.adoc[vertical slices] is the furthest from a workflow, because it deliberately has no centre;
converting it later means gathering the slices back into one flow. A process written as
xref::state-from-process-events.adoc[state from its own events] is the closest, because it already records its own
progress as facts, which is much the same information a workflow persists on your behalf.

If you expect to adopt workflows later, that is an argument for keeping the process in one class now.
